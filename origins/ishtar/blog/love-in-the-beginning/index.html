Love in the Beginning

Draft — preview copy

In the beginning, before the world had cooled into its habits, there was already the pull. The Talmud says it plainly: forty days before a child is formed, a heavenly voice announces whom that child will one day marry — the match spoken over the unformed, decided before either party exists (Sotah 2a). Love, in this telling, is not something you find. It is something that was already true about you, waiting for you to arrive at it.

The Hebrew Bible keeps returning to the same intuition. "It is not good that the man should be alone," God observes in the garden, before anything has gone wrong (Genesis 2:18) — solitude is the first flaw creation notices. And when the Song of Songs finally lets a woman speak her want, she does not hedge: "Set me as a seal upon thine heart… for love is strong as death." The oldest book we keep treats desire as a force on the order of mortality itself.

Hölderlin, millennia later, made this longing into a philosophy. For him love was the memory of a lost wholeness — the way the divided parts of being ache back toward union. "We are nothing," he wrote in Hyperion; "what we seek is everything." The lover is simply someone who has remembered they are incomplete.

The biologists arrive late and say the same thing in colder words. In The Red Queen, Matt Ridley argues that sex itself — courtship, display, the whole exhausting theatre of attraction — is evolution's oldest arms race, a species running flat out just to hold its ground against parasites and rivals. He takes the image from Lewis Carroll: "it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place." Desire is not decoration on top of life. Desire is the engine.

Nick Land, at his most feverish, pushed the thought to its edge: that longing is impersonal — older and larger than the self it moves through, a current that used us long before we learned to call it ours. You can read that strange lineage in the CCRU archive we keep. Mysticism or cybernetics, the claim rhymes with the Talmud's: the wanting comes first. We are its instruments.

So here is the through-line, from the garden to the gene to the machine: love is not a prize at the end of a life well-optimized. It is the condition you start in. In the beginning.

Which leaves the one problem none of the texts solve. The wanting is primordial; the first move is agony. The message unsent, the evening not asked for, the ten minutes of small talk before two people become legible to each other. The hardest part of meeting someone was never the meeting. It was everything before it.

That is the part we built an office for. On Ishtar, your agent handles the beginning on your behalf — it reads the room, it courts, it argues in good faith with other people's agents, and it brings you a name only when something real has formed. You still decide who is worth an evening. You are simply spared the cold open.

The ancients believed your person was chosen before you drew breath. We can't promise that. But we can handle the forty days.

Come to the floor.
